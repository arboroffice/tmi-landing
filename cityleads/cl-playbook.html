1 Who You Target
The Right Person
The business owner who signs checks
Not the office manager. Not the foreman. Not the receptionist who says "I'll pass along your info." You are looking for the person who made the decision to hire their last employee, buy their last truck, and open their last bank account.

That person is usually not sitting at a front desk. They're out back, in the shop, on the phone, or pulling up in a pickup. Learn how to ask for them without triggering the gatekeeper: "Is the owner around? I'm local — just want to introduce myself." Short. Unthreatening. Direct.
Ideal Profile
10–50 employees, physical operations, the trades
The sweet spot is a company that has grown past "just me and a helper" but hasn't yet hired a full operations department. They're busy enough to have real problems — scheduling conflicts, job costing headaches, crew accountability issues — but small enough that the owner still feels every one of them personally.

Best industries: HVAC, plumbing, roofing, electrical, concrete, painting, landscaping, heavy equipment rental, pipeline, welding shops. The owner has calluses. There are trucks in the lot. It smells like work.
⚠️ Warning Signs — Avoid or Deprioritize
Too large: 100+ employees usually means they already have a system or a dedicated ops person who gatekeeps decisions.

Just started: Under 2 years old and under 5 employees — not enough pain yet. Come back in 18 months.

Not the decision maker: If the person you met says "I'd have to run it by my partner / owner / boss" — that's fine once. But if you can't get to the actual decision maker after two visits, move on.

Franchise location: They often can't adopt new software without corporate approval. Rare exceptions exist.
2 Walking In
First 10 Seconds
What to say when you walk through the door
You have one job in the first 10 seconds: not sound like a salesperson. The moment you say "software," "platform," or "solution," the owner's defenses go up. Lead with who you are and why you're local.
"Hey — I'm [Name], I work with a company out of [City] called TMI. We work with trade companies in this area. Is the owner around for two minutes? I'm not trying to sell anything today — just want to introduce myself and see what you're working on."
If they say the owner isn't around: "No problem — is there a good time to catch them? I'll swing back." Get a time. Write it down. Show up.
🚫 What NOT to say
Never say "AI" in a cold walk-in. You'll lose them in 3 words.

Never say "software" — it sounds like another app they have to pay for and nobody uses.

Never say "system" in the first visit. You haven't earned the right to talk systems yet.

Never say "I was just in the area" — it sounds like you're making excuses for showing up. Own it: "I'm out meeting businesses in the area this week."
Before You Leave
How to get the owner's number before you walk out
Don't leave without a direct number. Not the front desk number. Not a business card. The owner's cell.

After 5–10 minutes of actual conversation, ask: "What's the best way to reach you directly if I have a question? Can I grab your number?"

If they give you a business card, look at it and ask: "Is this your direct line, or should I text you on something else?"

You don't need to schedule anything on the first visit. Just get the number and tell them you'll be in touch.
3 What to Listen For
Pain Signals
Phrases that mean they're ready
These are real signals. When you hear them, stop talking and ask a follow-up question.
"We're just trying to keep up right now."
Capacity pain
"We lose track of where jobs are sometimes."
Visibility pain
"I still have to chase my guys for paperwork."
Admin pain
"We're growing but the margins aren't keeping up."
Job costing pain
"I spend half my day on the phone dispatching."
Dispatch pain
"Invoicing takes forever. We're always slow to collect."
Cash flow pain
Note Taking
How to take notes without making it weird
Don't take notes in front of them on the first visit. You'll look like you're filling out a form, and that changes the dynamic.

Instead: right after you leave — before you get to your car — pull out your phone and voice-memo or type exactly what they said. Get it in the app before you forget the specifics.

Specific quotes are gold. "He said invoicing takes them 2 weeks and he's still doing it himself on Sunday nights" is ten times more useful than "billing issues."
The Three Questions
Ask these and then stop talking
1. "What's the biggest thing slowing you down right now?"
Open-ended. No leading. Just listen. Nod. Let them finish.

2. "How are you handling [thing they just mentioned] right now?"
Gets them to describe their current process — which is where the gap between today and what TMI does becomes obvious.

3. "If that problem went away tomorrow, what would change for you?"
This is the closer. Gets them to describe the value of fixing it in their own words. Those words become your follow-up.
4 When It's Warm
Definition
What "warm" actually means
Warm is not "they were nice to me." Warm is not "they took my card." Warm is when the owner said something like:

"Yeah, that sounds like something we deal with." or "That would actually be really useful for us." or "How does that work exactly?"

If they asked a question about how it works, they're warm. If they complained about a specific problem and leaned in when you described what TMI does — warm. If they gave you their personal cell without hesitation — warm.
Second Visit
How to book the second visit before you leave the first
The single most important thing you can do at the end of a warm first visit is book the next step before you walk out.

"I'd love to come back and show you what this actually looks like for a company your size. Would [day] or [day] work to grab 20 minutes?"

Don't say "I'll follow up." That puts it on you to chase. Lock in a time with both of you in the room, right now. Even if it's two weeks out. A booked second visit is infinitely more valuable than a "sounds good, reach out."
Hand Off Trigger
When to bring in Mia or Tyler
Hand off to the TMI team when: the owner has agreed to an audit or demo, OR they've asked pricing questions, OR they've mentioned a specific timeline ("we're making decisions next quarter").

Do NOT wait until everything is perfect. You hand off warm, not closed. Your job is to get them to the door — the team closes.

When in doubt: fill out the handoff form and let the team decide. There's no penalty for handing off too early. There's a real cost to holding on too long.
5 Posting Without Looking Fake
What to Post
Content that builds credibility on the ground
The best content you can post is documentation of real work — yours and theirs.

Job sites: A photo of a crew working, a freshly poured pad, a rooftop at sunrise. Caption: "Another day on the ground in [City]. These guys don't quit." No tags required. No pitch.

Owner conversations (no names): "Talked to an HVAC owner this week who's been doing his invoices manually every Sunday night for 11 years. That's the problem we're here to fix." This is real. It's relatable. It builds trust.

Behind the scenes: You driving to an area, walking into a neighborhood, using the app. Makes it real without being an ad.
🚫 What NOT to Post
Never post client data — no business names without permission, no owner details, no deal sizes.

Never post pricing — not even ranges. Pricing conversations happen in person, with context.

Never share internal TMI info — company metrics, commission structures, client lists, or anything from this app.

Never post a pitch — "TMI is the best platform for trades" — nobody wants to read that. Show, don't tell.
6 Hand Off Rules
The Handoff Form
What the form needs — no shortcuts
A useful handoff has five things:

1. Business name and address — specific enough that the team can find it without Googling.

2. Owner's name and direct number — not the front desk. The person you actually talked to.

3. The specific pain they described — in their words, not yours. Copy from your visit notes.

4. What was agreed — "second visit booked for Tuesday 2pm" or "interested in audit, no date set yet."

5. Your read on urgency — hot, warm, or cold. One word. Be honest.
What Happens Next
After you submit the handoff
The TMI team will reach out to the business within 24 hours of a hot or warm handoff. You'll be looped in via text when they make contact.

You stay on the relationship. You're still the local face. If the owner texts you with a question, answer it. If they go quiet after the TMI team reaches out, let us know.

Your commission starts when the deal closes — not when you hand off. But a clean, complete handoff is the single biggest factor in whether the deal closes fast or stalls.
